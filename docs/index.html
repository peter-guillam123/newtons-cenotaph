Newton's cenotaph

A monument to Isaac Newton that was drawn, admired, and never built. This is a reconstruction from the six drawings that survive, which you can scroll through and then walk.

Scroll ↓

A sphere 150 metres across

Boullée wanted a monument with nothing on it. No columns, no statue, no inscription you could read from the ground. A sphere, because for him the sphere was the perfect form, and an enormous one: taller than the Great Pyramid, wider than St Paul's is high.

He called the design a cenotaph rather than a tomb because Newton's body was already in Westminster Abbey. This was to be an idea of Newton, at the scale of the idea.

The base

The sphere sits in a drum of masonry, cut open at the front so the globe seems to rest on the earth. Two terraces of cypresses, the Roman tree of mourning, ring it, with a third ring at ground level. A vaulted gallery runs inside the drum, visible only in the section drawing.

Boullée left no dimensions, so every proportion here is measured off his drawings. Where his own drawings disagree with each other, and they do, the elevations won.

The way in

A single small arch at the foot of the sphere. The visitor is meant to be dwarfed before being enclosed, then to pass through a long tunnel into the dark.

The tunnel

The tunnel runs about thirty metres through the shell of the sphere. Boullée drew it as a black hole in a wall of light; the drama was in the contrast.

The tomb

It opens at the bottom of the sphere, onto a mound with Newton's sarcophagus on top. That is the only object in the whole interior. Boullée wrote that he wanted to surround Newton with his own discovery, which meant putting him inside the sky.

Day: a sky made of holes

This is the trick. Thousands of holes pierce the shell, so by day the sun comes through as points and the visitor stands under a night sky at noon. Boullée drew the constellations in on the section. It is, in effect, a planetarium about 140 years early.

Night: the lamp

After dark the holes go black and an armillary sphere hung at the centre is lit instead: a sun in the middle of Newton's universe. Boullée labelled the two drawings effet de jour and effet de nuit.

Whether one lamp could light a stone sphere 150 metres across is a fair question. He was not a man to be stopped by a fair question.

Walk it

Take control and go where you like. Keyboard on a desktop, two joysticks on a phone, and shortcuts to the forecourt, the terraces, the tomb and the vault.

The model follows the drawings; where they run out (the stairs round the sphere, the door itself) I have guessed. The About section below says where.

About

This is a 3D reconstruction of Étienne-Louis Boullée's Cénotaphe à Newton of 1784, one of the most reproduced architectural drawings ever made and one of the least built. It was modelled in Blender, in conversation with Claude Code, from the six drawings held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France: a plan, two perspective elevations (day and night), two sections (day effect and night effect) and an interior view. The renders on this page are Cycles; the walkthrough is the same model exported to the browser and lit in real time, which is why it looks cleaner and a bit less atmospheric than the stills.

Boullée gave no dimensions. The literature agrees on a sphere of about 150 metres, so I fixed that and measured everything else as a proportion of the sphere's radius off the drawings: the drum at 1.55 radii across and half a radius high, the upper tier at 1.25 radii, the cypress rings at 1.1, 1.4 and 1.9 radii, the shell five metres thick. The full table is in the project's README.

Where I had to choose

  • The two sections draw the drum wider relative to the sphere than the two elevations do, roughly 1.77 radii against 1.57. The plan sides with the elevations, so I did too.
  • The elevations show the sphere sitting in a concave cradle with the front cut open. The sections show masonry hugging the sphere with no gap. I modelled the cradle, because that is what you would see.
  • The plan sketches curving stairs round the sphere. They are not modelled. The monumental stair up the front of the upper tier is.
  • The stars are a material trick (glowing points on the inner surface), not several thousand real holes. Boullée would probably have approved of the economy.
  • The cypresses are about 1,900 instanced spindles. Botanists should look away.

How the page works

Scrolling drives a camera along a path through the building. "Walk" hands you the camera: WASD or arrow keys, Shift to hurry, F to fly, N for night, X for the section, the number keys 1 to 5 to jump between viewpoints, Escape to get out. On a phone the two joysticks do the same and the buttons stay in the top bar. "Section" cuts the building in half along the line Boullée used for his sections. Day and night switch between his two lighting effects. It all runs on three.js with no build step, so the source is readable as it stands.
